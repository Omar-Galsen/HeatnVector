Bernoulli equation

Think of flowing water as carrying an energy budget. At each point it has pressure energy, kinetic energy from motion, and gravitational energy from height. In an ideal pipe with no pump or friction, their sum stays constant along a streamline. When one form increases, another must decrease.

P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgz₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgz₂
  • P represents pressure energy per unit volume: energy supplied by pushing the fluid.
  • ½ρv² is kinetic energy per unit volume: faster water carries more of it.
  • ρgz is gravitational energy per unit volume: higher water carries more of it.

Every term has units of J/m³ = Pa; divide by 1000 to display kPa. In the example at right, narrowing the pipe increases velocity, so some pressure energy becomes kinetic energy. Raising the outlet also takes energy from pressure.

Why does the outlet speed change?

Model assumption: incompressible water

Incompressible means the water density stays approximately constant as it flows. The water does not shrink enough in this example to fit through a narrow opening. For steady flow in one pipe with no leaks or storage, the same volume must cross every section each second.

ρA₁v₁ = ρA₂v₂  →  A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ = Q

Density cancels because it is constant. Narrowing the outlet reduces area, so velocity rises to carry the same flow rate Q. Bernoulli then tells us how pressure energy trades with that increased motion and any change in height.

This is a good approximation for liquid water at the ordinary pressures shown. If the model predicts pressure near water’s vapor pressure, cavitation can occur and the single-phase liquid assumption breaks down.

Water is nearly incompressible. In steady flow through one pipe, the volumetric flow rate must be the same at the inlet and outlet: Q = A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. If the outlet has less area, the same amount of water must pass through a smaller opening each second, so it moves faster there.

For a round pipe, A = πD²/4, so v₂ = v₁(D₁/D₂)². Diameter is squared: reducing a 10 cm outlet to 7 cm gives an area of (7/10)² = 0.49 times the inlet area. With v₁ = 1.00 m/s, v₂ = 1/0.49 = 2.04 m/s. Halving the diameter gives one-quarter the area and four times the speed.

Keywords: what changes in Bernoulli?

Bigger outlet diameter → bigger area → slower outlet speed → smaller kinetic term (½ρv²) → higher pressure term (P) if height stays the same.

Smaller outlet diameter → smaller area → faster outlet speed → larger kinetic term (½ρv²) → lower pressure term (P).

Higher outlet → larger height term (ρgz) → lower pressure when speed stays the same.

In this simulator, changing inlet pressure or outlet height does not change v₂, because inlet speed and the two diameters are held fixed. Those sliders change the energy balance and the calculated outlet pressure. In a real system where a pump or pressure difference sets the flow rate, changing pressure can also change the flow speed.
